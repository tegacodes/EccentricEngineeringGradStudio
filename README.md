## Course Description
Critical design uses the language and tools of design to ask questions rather than solve problems. It makes the familiar strange, renders the invisible legible, and proposes alternatives — not because they are convenient, but because imagining and building them changes what is seen as possible.

This 7-week course draws on that tradition but applies it to networked infrastructure and engineering — what we call **eccentric engineering**. Not engineering as optimization, but engineering as argument. The working hypothesis is that powerful works of design often do not just reveal broken systems but are themselves systems that run differently. This course is about critically engaging with function, and implementing critique through new propositions.

The course moves through three modes:
1. **Reading infrastructure**
2. **Defamiliarizing through making**
3. **Realizing alternates**

## Three Critical Methods

These are the analytical and making tools carried through the whole course. In project documentation, students identify which method is doing what work.

- **Defamiliarization** — making a familiar system strange enough to see clearly. Used in reading exercises and documentation throughout.
- **Inversion** — identifying a dominant assumption and engineering its opposite. The generative move in every brief: find the assumption, build the refusal.
- **The Functional Diegetic Prototype** — build a system or intervention . Unlike conventional critical design, the prototype is not an object that *represents* a system — it *is* the system. It acts in the world and its critique is inseparable from its function.

## Course & Program Goals

### Program Goals (IDM)
- Develop conceptual thinking skills to generate ideas and content
- Develop a research and studio practice through inquiry and iteration
- Develop collaboration skills for teams and groups
- Develop critical thinking to position work within cultural, historical, aesthetic, economic, and technological contexts

### Course Goals
Students will:
- Treat infrastructure as a carrier of politics — recognizing that pipes, protocols, grids, and standards encode decisions about who is served, excluded, or surveilled
- Examine the idea that infrastructure only becomes visible in breakdown — using deliberate malfunction, friction, or "glitch" as a design and analytic strategy rather than something to engineer away
- Develop an understanding of scale-shifting: how a critique that works at the object level (defamiliarization) has to be restaged at the level of systems, intervening in flows, thresholds, and feedback loops
- Develop projects that are systems, using process as medium, and question notions of efficiency and optimization
- Use engineering and design as a rhetorical and philosophical medium rather than a problem-solving tool — producing artifacts that pose questions instead of resolving needs
- Locate work in relation to conceptual art, experimental arts/engineering practices, sci-fi, and critical theory — rather than HCI/UX conventions
- Think critically about audience, content, form, and process
